The evaluation scores the document

The paperwork is a separate
object from the company.

An evaluator sits with a scoring template and your submission, and awards points for what is present, findable and specific. They cannot award points for what you know and did not write down. They are not permitted to.

A Norwegian road contractor argued the same thing from the other end. Mesta complained to KOFA that the authority had assessed how each supplier scored on the documentation requirements rather than on the award criterion itself, and had given weight to matters no documentation was ever asked for.

That is two countries, two industries, and the same complaint. It is not an accusation of unfairness. It is a description of the machine, and the machine is why the feedback you get back names the outcome and never the line.

Before you say it is all price anyway

Sometimes it is.

In Latvia between 2021 and 2023 the winner of 73% of procurements was picked on lowest price alone. If that is your market, stop reading. You are right and I have nothing to sell you.

Norway is the other end. Since 1 January 2024 contracting authorities there have had to weight climate and environmental criteria at a minimum of 30%, and in the first year around 75% of tenders carried it. A third of the score, decided on documents, before anyone opens the commercial envelope.

Most of the tenders you bid sit between those two. The honest question is not whether price matters. It is how much of the score was never about price, and whether you have ever seen that part written down.

If your losses are price losses and nothing else, no document changes the outcome, and this is the wrong page for you.

What a document standard is

Written once, properly,
in the form an evaluator scores.

The set of things your firm asserts about itself. Capability, process, references with the detail that makes them count, environmental and quality evidence stated as figures rather than adjectives.

It is not a management system. Nothing changes in the workshop. It belongs to you, your own people edit it, and the next tender starts from it instead of from nothing.

What it takes from you

Four hours.

Two conversations of about ninety minutes, and one read-through at the end.

You supply what already exists. Past submissions, certificates, drawings, whatever you sent last time. I do not need you to prepare anything, and I am not going to ask you to fill in a questionnaire so that I can sell you the answers.

If you have sat through a pre-tender dialogue and come away feeling you had just given a day of free consulting to someone who was never going to buy, you know why that sentence is in here.

We manage without them

They are not wrong.

A Swedish firm said that to a trade body survey, with pride rather than regret. We manage without them. We get paid in full by private clients, completely without hassle.

Public work argues about scope, pays on its own schedule, and costs you a week of unpaid writing before anyone looks at your price. A firm that walked away and filled the capacity with private customers made a sound decision, and nothing here says otherwise.

One number complicates it. The field is thinning, and it is thinning for the reason above. Firms are leaving because the paperwork became unreasonable, not because they stopped being able to do the work.

So the contest is getting easier to win at the same time as it is getting harder to enter. Everyone who withdraws hands their share to whoever can still produce the documents.

You bid three times a year. That will not change and it does not need to. A firm bidding constantly can absorb a bad submission, because there is another one next month. Yours carries a third of your public work, and the one you lose on a paperwork date is not replaced.

Bidding more is not the offer. The offer is that the few you do bid stop being lost on the part nobody explained to you.
